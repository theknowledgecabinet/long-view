Harris is gently answering that classical case for rule by the elite, and his faith in common sense stands comfortably beside Tocqueville''s trust in the self-governing wisdom of ordinary Americans. What makes the essay worth keeping is the humility it asks of both sides: we are cleverer than the experts suppose, and yet the world is complicated enough that no intelligence, however great, can take that complication away.'
bigger_heading: The bigger picture
other_side: Alexis de Tocqueville, who watched the young American democracy in the 1830s, looked at the same question from the other end. He was less afraid that the people were too stupid than that they were too much on their own, and that a majority left to itself could harden into a soft, paternal despotism that manages even willing adults for them. Harris is alert to the arrogance of the expert; Tocqueville is alert to the quiet power of the majority. Put together, the two suggest that a healthy democracy needs both a humble elite and citizens who stay tied to one another.
question: Next time a confident expert or a headline tells you what to think, can you notice the easier question you are answering in place of the harder, real one? What would it feel like to sit with the harder one for a while, without hurrying to a tidy answer?

In this essay Lee Harris takes on a fashionable complaint: that the ordinary voter is simply too unlearned to run a democracy, and that we would be better off leaving the hard choices to the experts. The argument comes from a team of psychologists at Cornell, who worry that most of us cannot tell a brilliant idea from a bad one. Harris thinks this gets the picture exactly backwards, and he has two moves.

His first is historical. Extraordinarily clever people have held extraordinarily dumb ideas: George Bernard Shaw, Sidney and Beatrice Webb, Thorstein Veblen and Jawaharlal Nehru all thought Stalin's Soviet Union was a genuine worker's paradise. So a high IQ is no protection against a bad idea. And when Harris asks what the men who voted for Andrew Jackson in 1828 could tell you about Einstein, Darwin, Keynes or the internet, the answer is nothing, because none of those things had come to pass yet. And still, as he points out, if the unintelligent were ever going to do democracy in, they would have done it long ago. The real danger the old elites feared was never the foolish mob, but the demagogue, a charismatic figure who seizes power by playing on people's ignorance, like the dictators of Greece and Rome. America's outstanding record, Harris argues, is its refusal to hand too much power to any single person, and the credit belongs to what he calls the "homespun pragmatism of ordinary Americans, familiarly known as common sense."

His second move is aimed at the modern expert. The wise men of the Greek world could recognise one another, but today's brilliant neurologist, whom Harris dubs Frank, knows nothing at all about economics. And when we face a question that is too hard, we do exactly what Daniel Kahneman describes: we quietly substitute an easier question for it. We ask whether a doctor is prestigious instead of whether the treatment works, in the lovely way Harris's mother asked "Did you like him?" rather than "Is he a good doctor?" The upshot is not that we should stop trusting anyone, but that the best hope for democracy still lies in the "unregulated marketplace of ideas", with the old caution that "Let the buyer beware."

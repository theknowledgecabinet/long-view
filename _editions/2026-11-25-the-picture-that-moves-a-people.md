bigger_picture: 'Sorel''s idea of the myth travelled far. He was influenced by Nietzsche, and his picture of the myth as a historical force was taken up by Hannah Arendt and by Adorno and Horkheimer of the Frankfurt School, who asked how an age of Enlightenment reason could produce such destruction. For readers following the themes Lee Harris cared about, the book is a masterclass in how fantasy and ideology work in the modern world: not as false arguments people happen to believe, but as pictures of the future that give effort its meaning and, in Sorel''s view, make compromise seem childish. It is a tool for understanding, whenever a movement is moving, whether the picture it carries is strong enough to bear the truth.'
bigger_heading: The bigger picture
other_side: 'Hannah Arendt approached the same question from the other end. In The Origins of Totalitarianism, written after the war, she took up Sorel''s idea of the myth and asked why such pictures grip people so completely. Her answer was that ideology becomes a force when common sense fails: when the world no longer makes sense to ordinary experience, people flee into a system that explains everything. Sorel saw the myth as the engine of the movement; Arendt asked about the wound that makes the myth needed. Yet the two agree on the deepest point: a shared image of history, once accepted, moves the people who carry it, and carries them whether they still mean what it said.'
question: Which pictures of the future do you carry, for your city, your work, your country or your own life? And which of them do you hold on to not because they are likely, but because they give your effort its meaning?

Georges Sorel was a French syndicalist thinker, and in Reflections on Violence, published in 1908, he set out to study what actually moves a social movement. Central to the book is his theory that what moves a movement is not argument or policy, but the myth. A myth, for Sorel, is not a falsehood but a vivid image, a "picture" held by a whole community, best understood as a whole rather than dissected. In his famous definition, "Men who are participating in a great social movement always picture their coming action as a battle in which their cause is certain to triumph." He did not care whether the picture was literally true; by his own words he was "not at all concerned to justify the perpetrators of violence", but to understand the function such images serve.

Sorel set the myth against the utopia, the grand scheme drawn up in advance by the intellectual. A utopia, he wrote, is "an intellectualist product": it conforms society to the intellect, works out each problem piecemeal, and then fits the results "into approaching legislation". The myth, by contrast, is spontaneous and felt as a whole, and too particular to any one culture to be described in general terms. His most telling example of a myth that was never literally true yet moved people for centuries is the Christian apocalyptic belief in coming punishment. "The catastrophe did not come to pass, but Christian thought profited so greatly from the apocalyptic myth", he notes. For Sorel that is exactly the point: "The myth must be judged as a means of acting on the present; any attempt to discuss how far it can be taken literally is devoid of sense."

To read Sorel fairly you have to stand in his moment. He wrote as the French left tore between parliamentary reformers, among them Jaurès and Clemenceau, whom he saw as careerists who waved the "specter of revolution" to frighten voters and win concessions, and syndicalists who wanted workers to organise themselves in their own groups and act. His own myth was the general strike, the spontaneous uprising of the working class, and for Sorel the good myth is the totalising one, the picture that "drags into the revolutionary track everything it touches". His own politics were extreme by any standard, and the book is a dangerous one to read uncritically. What lasts is not his programme but his diagnosis: how an image, once adopted by a people, gains a power over them stronger than any argument.

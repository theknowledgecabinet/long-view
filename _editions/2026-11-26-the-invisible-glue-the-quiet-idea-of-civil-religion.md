Look closely at any nation and you will find that it shares far more than laws and a language. There is a flag and an anthem, oaths of allegiance, monuments and memorial days, founding stories told with quiet reverence. Put these together and you get what scholars call civil religion: the "implicit religious values of a nation", expressed "through public rituals, symbols (such as the national flag), and ceremonies on sacred days and sacred places". It is distinct from the churches, though it can borrow from them: it belongs to the nation rather than to any one denomination, and it is usually practised by political leaders who are ordinary citizens, not clergy.

The idea began with Jean-Jacques Rousseau, who coined the phrase in 1762 in The Social Contract. He saw civil religion as "the moral and spiritual foundation essential for any modern society" and designed it to be "a form of social cement", helping to unify the state by providing it with sacred authority. He kept it deliberately minimal, a handful of tenets that should be "simple, few in number, and stated in precise words": a god, an afterlife in which virtue is rewarded and vice punished, and tolerance towards those who believe otherwise. Everything beyond that, he insisted, should be beyond the reach of government, left to each person's private conscience. A society, in his view, could ask for just enough shared reverence to knit its people together, without demanding a single creed.

The most memorable picture of this in action comes from the American sociologist Robert Bellah, writing in 1967. Looking at the United States, he noticed that the country had quietly assembled its own sacred world, with "its own prophets and its own martyrs, its own sacred events and sacred places, its own solemn rituals and symbols". George Washington stands as the story's Moses-like leader, the founding documents are treated almost like scripture, and with the Civil War came a new theme of "death, sacrifice and rebirth", expressed through the rituals of Memorial Day. Here is an entire civil religion: its feast days of Thanksgiving, Veterans Day and Memorial Day, its rituals of the flag salute and God Bless America, its founding story, all belonging to the country rather than to any church.

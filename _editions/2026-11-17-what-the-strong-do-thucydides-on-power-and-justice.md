The two stand together on purpose: the best image a city has of itself, and the worst thing it did, both preserved without flattery. That is the permanent question at the heart of civilisation and its enemies. A civilisation that defends itself on raw power alone, and takes power to be all there is, fears everything on the outside while weakening itself on the inside. More than two thousand years later, whenever a person or a state has to decide what to do when it is stronger than the other side, this is still the first scene worth remembering.'

other_side: Plato staged the same question very differently. In the first book of the Republic, a sharp tongue called Thrasymachus insists that justice is nothing but the advantage of the stronger, which is close to the line Thucydides puts in the mouth of the Athenian envoys. Socrates' long answer is that justice is worth its cost, even for the person who practises it. Plato and Thucydides, the two great writers of Athens, agree that the question cannot be dodged. Plato holds it in a room, Thucydides holds it in a city under siege. Plato offers an answer; Thucydides leaves the question hanging in the air, which is probably why his scene still has its force.

In the summer of 416 BC, the great Athenian empire sent at least 3,400 men and 38 ships to a small island in the Aegean called Melos. The Melians were Dorians, the same people as the Spartans, and they had stayed neutral in the long war between Athens and Sparta. Athens demanded that they join the Delian League, the alliance of cities led by Athens, and pay tribute, or be destroyed. They refused. Thucydides, who was in exile at the time, did not witness the negotiations before the siege, but he wrote them up as a dialogue, and it has become one of the most talked about scenes in the whole of Greek history.

What makes the scene unforgettable is that the Athenians offer no moral justification at all. Their position is stated plainly: "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." The Melians then give the case for the weak, and it is moving. They are a neutral city, not an enemy. An invasion would alarm the other neutral states. It would be shameful to submit without a fight. The gods will help them because their cause is just, and their kinsmen in Sparta may come to their rescue. To each hope the Athenians reply, calmly and almost persuasively. Sparta has too little at stake to intervene. The other Greek cities will not fight for a distant island. The gods, they say, side with the strong, because that is the natural order of things. And hope itself, they add, is a luxury: if Melos resists it will probably lose, and come to bitterly regret what the text calls their irrational optimism.

The Melians did not submit. Athens blockaded the city until the following winter, and the hunger was so extreme that "Melian famine" entered the Greek language as a word for desperate starvation. When Melos finally fell, the Athenians executed the men of fighting age and sold the women and children into slavery. Thucydides says the point of the conquest was to show the strength and sternness of Athens, and to discourage the cities of the empire from rebelling. It did not work: a few years later, after the disastrous expedition to Sicily, rebellions broke out all across the empire, and the massacre had shocked the Greek world, even in Athens. The lasting gift of the dialogue, then, is not only its lesson about power. It is the choice of the man who wrote it. A historian of Athens let his own city be heard at its most brutal, without a single softening word, so that anyone who reads it, in any age, can judge the question for themselves.

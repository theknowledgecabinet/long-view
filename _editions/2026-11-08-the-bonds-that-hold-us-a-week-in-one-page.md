Monday opened with Lee Harris and his favourite trick: pressing something strange into a familiar shape. He begins with Montezuma, who had no category for the Spanish and so read them as the returning god Quetzalcoatl, a mismatch that proved fatal. Harris turns the same question on 9/11 and names what he calls a fantasy ideology: a belief that works like a stage, where the believer plays the hero of a cause he already accepts and treats everyone else as a prop. The example that stayed with me is Italy's conquest of Ethiopia, a war with no economic or strategic prize, demanded by the fantasy itself rather than serving any real end.

Tuesday met a very different voice in Carl Schmitt, the German legal theorist. His claim is blunt: the specifically political distinction is the one between friend and enemy. What keeps it alive is that the enemy is a role, not a name. Language, ethnicity, culture or religion turns political the moment a group uses it to define itself and becomes willing to defend that identity by force, and because each group decides for itself what counts as a threat, no outside judge can settle a truly political conflict.

Wednesday went back to the fourteenth century and a Tunisian scholar, Ibn Khaldun, who had already given social cohesion a name: asabiyyah, which his translator Franz Rosenthal rendered as "group feeling." It is the shared bond that lets a tribe defend itself and act as one. His bold claim is that this bond is cyclical: strongest at the birth of a civilisation, under the pressure of nomadic life, then thinning out as a dynasty grows settled and comfortable, and at last loosening into faction until a newer group with a fresher bond rises from the periphery.

Thursday brought in Karl Popper as a counterpoint. He names the story that historicists tell, that history obeys discoverable laws and so the future is already written, and traces it back to the doctrine of a chosen people, its two great modern faces being racialism and Marxism. His answer is the open society, a society whose first duty is the freedom of its citizens, where problems are fixed one by one in what he calls piecemeal social engineering, because if the future is genuinely open, the whole weight of responsibility falls back on us.

Friday closed the week back with Harris, in a short interview where he was careful with his word. He chose "civilisation" because it is value-free, describing a state of social organisation marked by tolerance, cooperation, a lessening reliance on violence, and above all stability. What struck me is that he calls it a transgenerational project, a trust that reaches across generations, and his clearest example is the Soviet Union, where when the state stopped being willing to apply terror, there was, in his words, "nothing there to hold them together."

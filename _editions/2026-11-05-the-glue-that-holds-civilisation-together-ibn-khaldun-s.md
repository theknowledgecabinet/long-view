bigger_picture: 'This connects to a question Lee Harris returned to again and again: what actually holds a society together, beyond law or economics or shared language? Tocqueville noticed a similar fragility in American society, and Durkheim gave the opposite condition, anomie, a name for the feeling of being untethered from the group. Ibn Khaldun''s cycle, read generously, suggests that social cohesion is not a permanent feature of civilisation but something that must be renewed, generation by generation, or it quietly fades.'
bigger_heading: The bigger picture
other_side: 'Robert Putnam, writing in the 1990s, picked up the thread from a different end. His concept of social capital asked a more practical question: why do some communities trust each other and cooperate more easily than others? Where Ibn Khaldun described a natural cycle of rise and decay, Putnam was more interested in the institutions, habits and networks that can rebuild that trust within a society. The two perspectives complement each other: one describes the pattern, the other asks what can be done within it.'
question: Think about a group you belong to, a family, a community, a team, a generation of friends. What is the thing that actually binds it, the shared history, the mutual obligation, the quiet feeling that everyone is in it together? And what would happen if that bond were suddenly asked to hold a much heavier weight than it was built for?

Long before the phrase 'social cohesion' entered English political vocabulary, an Arab scholar in fourteenth century Tunisia had already given it a name. Ibn Khaldun, writing his great work the Muqaddimah in 1377, called it asabiyyah, a word that in Arabic simply means 'group cohesiveness'. In the nomadic world he knew from experience, it was the bond that made a tribe able to defend itself, to share its burdens, and to act as one. When his translator Franz Rosenthal rendered the term into English, he chose the apt phrase 'group feeling', and it captures what Ibn Khaldun meant: that shared sense of belonging and purpose that makes people stick together not because they are forced to, but because they feel they belong to one another.

Ibn Khaldun made a bold claim that this bond is not static but cyclical. It is strongest at the very beginning of a civilisation, when a people is still shaped by the hard demands of nomadic life and the loyalty that comes with it. As a dynasty settles, becomes wealthy and comfortable, that group feeling slowly thins out. The rulers, far from the pressures that forged them, grow lax, their coordination weakens, and the shared bond 'dissolves into factionalism and individualism'. Eventually, says Ibn Khaldun, a new group with a fresher, stronger asabiyyah will rise from the periphery and take their place. He even offered a rough rule of thumb: no dynasty generally lasts more than three generations, roughly forty years each, because the bond that made it is not handed down intact.

What makes the idea memorable is its honesty. Ibn Khaldun was not writing a flattering account. He was saying that the very comfort a civilisation builds for itself is the thing that slowly loosens the social ties that held it together, and that the people who will inherit it will be the ones who still feel that bond most strongly. It is a sober and rather beautiful observation, and it has been read and argued over ever since.

It is still worth carrying today because the word 'orientalism' now stands for any culture flattening another into a single contemptuous image, and the question it poses is always about our own pictures of other people. The great American essayist Lee Harris, who engaged with this very book, spent a career exposing the fictions and fantasies that keep civilisations apart, and Said is his sharpest tool for seeing how one civilisation pictures another, and what that picture quietly costs.
bigger_heading: The bigger picture
other_side: 'Lee Harris turned his gaze mostly on the West and its own self-image, the myths and ideologies it uses to picture itself. Said looked at the same question from the other end: not the West''s picture of itself, but its picture of the East, and how that image became a weapon of empire. Each is useful where the other is blind. Harris shows you how a civilisation fictions its own story; Said shows you how the fictions of a dominant civilisation reach out and fix the people it rules. Both, generously read, are asking the same hopeful question: what happens when we set the fictions aside, and actually see one another?'
question: The pictures you carry of other places and peoples come from news, films and stories, most of them made by others. Do those pictures help you see those people more clearly, or do they flatten them into a single caricature?

In 1978, a public intellectual named Edward Said did something small that turned out to be enormous: he made the word 'Orientalism' a critical term. It was the name he gave to the way the Western world, through its writers and scholars, habitually pictured the Eastern world, Asia and North Africa, as primitive, irrational and inferior. That, he argued, was not innocent. The scholarly study of the East was 'inextricably tied to the imperialist societies that produced it', which made much of it a tool of power as well as knowledge.

Said saw three tangled meanings in the word: an academic field; a style of thought that divided the world into 'the Orient' and 'the Occident'; and, above all, a 'powerful political instrument of domination'. The clearest example is his line about the modern West: 'it is only a slight overstatement to say that Moslems and Arabs are essentially seen as either oil suppliers or potential terrorists.' The 'human density' and 'passion' of real life had, he said, been replaced by 'a series of crude, essentialized caricatures' that left the world 'vulnerable to military aggression'.

The deepest part of the idea is its honesty about knowledge. Said held that 'pure knowledge' is simply not possible, because every kind of knowing is shaped by the position and power of the knower. 'If the knowledge of Orientalism has any meaning, it is in being a reminder of the seductive degradation of knowledge, of any knowledge, anywhere, at any time.' His evidence for how these pictures are fictions was the 1973 war and oil embargo: when the 'East' acted with strength and resolve, it shattered the very image that had long described it as weak.

bigger_picture: 'Schmitt drew on the line of thought running from Hobbes: a state is legitimate chiefly in so far as it can protect its members, and a state that can no longer name its enemies can no longer do that. Lee Harris made a cousin of the same move: his book Civilization and Its Enemies asks what happens when the ties that hold a civilisation together fray, and where its real enemies lie. Both thinkers are, in the end, diagnosticians. They want us to see that the groups we live in are real, that they have boundaries, and that the state of those boundaries decides more of our daily life than we like to admit.'

other_side: 'From the other end, the fourteenth-century Tunisian historian Ibn Khaldun saw the same dynamic. He argued that a group''s social cohesion, the bond he called asabiyyah, is forged in opposition: communities rise when they are united against others, and decay when that bond relaxes. Where Schmitt was diagnosing how modern states think about themselves, Khaldun was describing how empires rise and fade over centuries. Read together, the two help us see that the enemy is not only a threat to a group, but part of what makes it.'

Schmitt's central claim is memorable for its bluntness: "the specific political distinction … is that between friend and enemy." For him, the political is its own sphere, reducible to none of the others. Ethics sorts the good from the bad, the aesthetic the beautiful from the ugly, the economic the profitable from the unprofitable, but the political distinction belongs to none of these. Its measure is "the utmost degree of intensity … of an association or dissociation": on one side, the willingness to fight and die together with members of your own group; on the other, the willingness to kill simply because someone belongs to a hostile one.

What makes the idea durable is that the enemy is a role, not a name. The political is not tied to any particular marker. Language, ethnicity, culture, religion: any of them acquires political quality the moment a group uses it to define itself and becomes willing to defend that identity against others by force. If one distinction stops dividing people, another latches on and takes its place. And because each group decides for itself what counts as a threat to its way of life, a truly political conflict cannot be settled by an outside judge. It has to be lived through.

That last point carries the weight of his critique of liberalism. Schmitt thought the liberal hope, that all conflicts could be solved to everyone's advantage by civilisation, technology and amicable compromise, would leave life shallow and purposeless, and that a community no longer willing to draw a line between friends and enemies would wither in internal strife or be overwhelmed by one that still could.

Steven Pinker's 2011 book opens with a claim that sounds like good news: violence in the world has declined on both the short and the long time scales, and the present is probably the most peaceful period in the history of the human species. He supports it with a great deal of data covering military conflict, homicide, genocide, torture, criminal justice and even the treatment of children. The title comes from the ending of Abraham Lincoln's first inaugural address, and Pinker uses the phrase as a metaphor for four motivations in us that "orient us away from violence and towards cooperation and altruism": empathy, self-control, the moral sense and reason.

His more interesting claim is about where the change came from. It is not, he argues, that human nature changed in any biological sense, but that the cultural and material world shifted in ways that give our peaceable motives the upper hand. He names five historical forces. The Leviathan: the modern state and courts, "with a monopoly on the legitimate use of force", which break the cycle of revenge. Commerce: the exchange of goods and services, which means that "other people become more valuable alive than dead". Feminization: the growing respect for the interests and values of women. Cosmopolitanism: literacy, mobility and mass media, which expand the circle of sympathy. And the "Escalator of Reason", a metaphor he credits to Peter Singer, which reframes "violence as a problem to be solved rather than a contest to be won". His clearest example is the civilizing process: between the late Middle Ages and the twentieth century, European countries saw a tenfold-to-fiftyfold decline in their rates of homicide.

What keeps the book honest is its humility. Pinker reminds us that "The decline, to be sure, has not been smooth; it has not brought violence down to zero; and it is not guaranteed to continue." He also notes the paradox that our impression of violence has not tracked this decline, perhaps because increased communication makes every incident visible. Progress, in his telling, is not a property of the species. It is the product of institutions and habits that can be maintained, or lost.

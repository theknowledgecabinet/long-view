bigger_picture: 'This is the quiet centre of Harris''s whole project, the question of how a liberal society comes into existence and keeps existing. He gently pushes back on the assumption that when people get together, the first thing they do is construct a nice little society. That idea, civilisation as a deliberate and hard-won achievement that must be renewed by each generation, lands him in the company of the older thinkers who saw social order as something built rather than inherited. It is a sober and hopeful note for anyone who feels trust and shared institutions running thin: cohesion is a thing you make, not a thing you are born into.'

other_side: 'Ibn Khaldun saw something similar from the other end. The fourteenth-century historian wrote about asabiyyah, the social solidarity that binds a group together, and watched groups rise when that bond was strong and falter when it loosened. Both Harris and Ibn Khaldun are pointing at the same quiet fact: a society is only as durable as the bonds that hold it. Harris helps us see what a civilisation is; Ibn Khaldun helps us see how those bonds come and go.'

Harris starts by being careful with his word. He chose "civilization" because it is value-free, not a banner for us against them. He defines it as a state of social organization marked by tolerance, by cooperation, by a lessening reliance on violence as a way of getting things done, and, most importantly, by stability. It is a picture of a society that has grown calmer rather than louder.

What strikes you is that he calls it a transgenerational project. A civilisation is not a snapshot; it is something you pass on. The values of your grandchildren should more or less reflect your own, and you hope they will not fall below the ethical baseline you set. There is real warmth in that: civilisation is trust that reaches across generations.

His best example comes from the collapse of the Soviet Union. The only reason to obey the orders there, he says, was fear of terror. When the state stopped being willing to apply terror, "there was nothing there to hold them together." The lesson he lands on is that a society has to have the ability to control its people in order to survive, and that the glue is far more fragile than most people assume.

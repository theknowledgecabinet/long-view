other_side: Émile Durkheim, one of the founders of sociology, spent much of his career asking what creates the "we" in a society. He argued that it is shared rituals and collective experience that forge the solidarity which holds people together. Haidt's hive switch, with its dancing, moving, and singing in unison, is close to a modern restatement of Durkheim's insight that the group is born in the moments when people do something together. Where Harris keeps a wary eye on the power of the group, Durkheim and Haidt see the group as the soil in which community grows.

Haidt's first and most surprising claim is that our beliefs are driven primarily by intuition, with reason operating mostly to justify what we already feel. He calls this social intuitionism, and tells it with a memorable metaphor, the "Rider and the Elephant". The elephant is the vast, unconscious mind that in fact determines the direction, while the rider is the rational mind that thinks it is in charge and then works up explanations for decisions it never made. He is in good company: he points to David Hume and the biologist E. O. Wilson as thinkers who gave reason a "relatively low estimation", in contrast with Lawrence Kohlberg and his stages of moral reasoning, which put reason at the centre.

In the second part he lays out moral foundations theory: just as a tongue is organised to respond to different sorts of food, the human brain is organised to respond to several distinct kinds of moral violation. Haidt identifies six, the care or harm, the fairness or cheating, the loyalty or betrayal, the authority or subversion, the sanctity or degradation, and the liberty or oppression. In an interview he summed it up by saying morality "is at least six things, and probably a lot more than that". The practical upshot is a kind of compassion: because Republicans and Democrats tend to focus on different moral receptors, their views can look strange to one another, and Haidt says this leads to worse political tactics and worse decision making.

In the last part he introduces a hypothetical "hive switch", a capacity that turns a selfish human "chimp" into a "groupish" human "bee". Cultures and organisations, he notes, have techniques for making people identify with their groups, such as dancing, moving, and singing in unison. And there is a warmly personal note in the book: Haidt has been a liberal all his life, and working on these questions he has become more open to other points of view.

bigger_picture: 'The paradox grew out of an older conversation. Plato had noticed a similar knot, arguing that unlimited freedom would make the bully free to enslave the meek, and proposing as the fix a wise ruler rather than the messy rule of the people. Popper answered with something braver: the institutions of liberal democracy, free to criticise and correct themselves, are how a society defends its own openness. More than eighty years on, the same question keeps appearing, in debates over what social platforms may take down, in European constitutions, and in any community that wants to stay open to ideas without being swallowed by the worst of them. It sits neatly beside Lee Harris''s thought on civilisation and its enemies: the open society is the one that learns to defend its openness without closing it.'
bigger_heading: The bigger picture
other_side: John Rawls saw the same question from the other end in A Theory of Justice (1971). A just society, he argued, must tolerate the intolerant, because a society that refused tolerance would itself be intolerant, and therefore unjust. He conceded, though, that if a society's security and the institutions of liberty were genuinely in danger, it could act in self-preservation. Starting from justice rather than from survival, Rawls lands very close to Popper's line, which is one of the things that makes the paradox so enduring.

The paradox of tolerance is a question the philosopher Karl Popper named in his 1945 book The Open Society and Its Enemies. If a free society is genuinely tolerant, must it tolerate those who are not? Popper's answer, in one memorable line, is that unlimited tolerance must lead to the disappearance of tolerance. A society that tolerates the intolerant without limit is, he warns, a society that will eventually be destroyed, and tolerance with it.

But Popper is careful, and his answer is far more restrained than the slogan suggests. He does not think intolerant ideas should be shut down at once. As long as they can be answered with reason and kept in check by public opinion, he writes, suppression would certainly be most unwise. The danger is the movement that will not play by those rules: it may begin by denouncing all argument and forbid its followers to listen to it. For such a movement, he argues, we should claim, in the name of tolerance, the right not to tolerate the intolerant.

In Germany this idea became law after the war. The country's constitution embodies what is called defensive democracy: if fundamental rights such as free speech or the right to assemble are used to fight the democratic order, they can be forfeited, and political parties that seek to abolish it can be banned. It is one of the clearest places where a democracy has written its answer to Popper's paradox into its own founding rules.

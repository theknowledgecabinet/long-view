Monday opened with Jonathan Haidt and his memorable Rider and the Elephant. Our intuitions decide first, and reason arrives afterwards to tidy up the explanation. He then lays out moral foundations, six distinct receptors (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity and liberty) through which different groups read the world. The upshot is a kind of compassion: because different sides attend to different receptors, their views can look strange to one another.

Tuesday asked whether a free society must tolerate those who would destroy it. Popper's answer, in his 1945 book The Open Society and Its Enemies, was far more restrained than the slogan suggests. He did not want to shut down intolerant ideas while they could still be answered with reason and kept in check; what he wanted to refuse was the movement that will not play by those rules. In postwar Germany the idea became law: rights used to fight the democratic order can be forfeited, and parties that seek to abolish it can be banned.

Wednesday brought Steven Pinker's long arc. Violence, he argues, has declined across the long run of history, and the title comes from Abraham Lincoln's phrase for the better angels of our nature: empathy, self-control, the moral sense and reason. The change did not come from human nature itself but from institutions, the state, commerce, the growing respect for women's values, cosmopolitanism, and the escalator of reason that reframes violence as a problem to be solved. His clearest example is the tenfold to fiftyfold decline in European homicide rates since the late Middle Ages, and he keeps the book honest by noting that the decline was uneven, incomplete and not guaranteed.

Thursday closed with William James and the man walking round a squirrel in the mountains, while the whole camping party squabbled over the metaphysics of it. James's answer was to ask what practical difference the question made to your life, and the quarrel dissolved. That is the pragmatic method, primarily a way of settling disputes that otherwise might be interminable, and it extends its generosity even to grand idealist systems. A new truth, he says, grows much as a tree grows, grafting itself on to the ancient body of what we already believe.

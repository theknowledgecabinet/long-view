Before the First World War, men like the American orator William Jennings Bryan called themselves pacifists, but the word meant something different to them than to us. It did not mean refusing to fight under any circumstances. It meant refusing to fight for selfish national aims, while accepting that a community of nations would sometimes have to use force to defend the rules against those who broke them. Harris calls them pragmatic pacifists, because, in his phrase, "they wanted something imperfect that worked rather than something perfect that didn't."

Their flaw was one Harris spots with a sharp eye. If the community of nations is to keep the rules, someone has to keep them with an army, and no nation would let a foreign agent decide when and where its children might be called to die for it. Harris thinks this was predictable on a priori grounds, as he puts it, the grounds of pure reasoning without needing to look at experience, because it is impossible to imagine a world where people's "native xenophobia" had been cut down to zero, and it is just as permanent a part of human nature, he jokes, as the need to sleep. So the perfect peace of a world police was never going to be the peace that anyone would actually live under.

What was new, in the Gulf War of 1991, was that for once a nation, the United States, volunteered to be the army that enforced the moral sentiment of the world, and Harris notes that the disinterestedness of this was proved by the fact that it was left unfinished the first time around, because if the United States had been after the oil, why don't we have it now? Once it was in that role, it faced a moral quandary that no one had planned for. The war was fought for the sake of international law, so the question no one had ever asked now had to be answered. On what conditions could the United States act for the world, and what happens to a country's leadership once a tyrant has been vanquished in a war he brought about himself? Harris's memorable close is that a great power has only three ways to relate to the international community, "either as its lackey, or as its leader, or as its tyrant". The world cannot really expect the first, and it certainly doesn't want the third. So it is left with one choice.

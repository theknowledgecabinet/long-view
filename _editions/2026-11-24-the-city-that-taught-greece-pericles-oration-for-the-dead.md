bigger_picture: 'This is one of the oldest examples of what later thinkers would call a civil religion: the shared story a city tells about itself to hold itself together. Lee Harris returned to this question in his work on the civil religion of liberal societies, suggesting that a free people lives partly on the shared fictions it tells itself, and that those fictions need renewing as the world changes. Pericles, speaking at the moment his city''s confidence most needed support, shows how gently such a story can be told: it honours the dead, flatters the living, and asks for nothing in return but courage.'

other_side: 'Plato saw the same question from a more sceptical side. In the Republic he suggests that a city needs a "noble lie", a founding story deliberately woven by its wise rulers to bind the people together. Where Pericles speaks openly to a democracy, describing a city as it is, Plato imagines a story carefully shaped for stability. Yet the two agree on the deepest point: a city lives on the story it tells about itself, and both knew that when the story fails, the city is in trouble.'

In the late fifth century BC, when Athens was at war, the city held an annual public funeral for all those who had died in battle. Their remains lay in a tent for three days, then were carried in a procession in ten cypress coffins, one for each of the Athenian tribes, and an empty one for the missing and unrecovered. At the end of the first year of the Peloponnesian War, the speaker chosen to address the mourners was Pericles, the leading citizen of the city, and what he said survives in book two of Thucydides' History of the Peloponnesian War.

Pericles begins with a modesty that is almost charming. The reputations of the brave dead, he says, should "not be imperilled in the mouth of a single individual". Then he does something no other Athenian funeral speech seems to have done, a move that the scholar David Cartwright captures when he calls the speech "a eulogy of Athens itself". Pericles skips over the martial achievements his audience would have expected, choosing instead to praise the city: "the road by which we reached our position, the form of government under which our greatness grew". He speaks of laws that afford "equal justice to all", of neighbours living "far from exercising a jealous surveillance over each other", and of advancement that falls to capacity rather than birth. The climax is one of the most famous lines in all of political writing: "as a city we are the school of Hellas".

The final turn of the speech is the heart of it. Pericles ties the city's greatness to the dead: the Athens he has celebrated is only what the heroism of these men has made it. Then he addresses the survivors directly. "So died these men as becomes Athenians", he says, "you, their survivors, must determine to have as unfaltering a resolution in the field". The funeral is thus also a mirror: by telling the city who it is, the speech asks the living to live up to that image.

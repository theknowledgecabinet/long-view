Harris opens with a story that belongs to no single nation or century. When the Spanish ships reached the coast of Mexico, Montezuma had no ready category for white men with steel and strange intentions. The only story his world offered was a myth, the return of the white god Quetzalcoatl, so that is what he expected, and the mismatch proved fatal. Harris's point is gentler than it first sounds: when an event is strange to us, we instinctively press it into our own familiar categories. The question he asks of the attacks of 9/11 is whether we were making exactly that mistake, reading a baffling act through the Clausewitzian idea that war is simply politics carried on by other means, a rational calculation with an end in view.

The essay's answer is a phrase of his own: a fantasy ideology. Harris first encounters it in a personal memory. In college in the late sixties, a friend planned to join a massively disruptive protest against the Vietnam War, and when Harris noted that it would likely backfire, the friend replied that it was, in his words, good for his soul. The demonstration was not about changing anyone's mind. It was a stage on which the protester played the hero of a cause he already believed in, and the commuters he lay down in front of existed only as props in the performance. Harris likens it to a game of Dungeons and Dragons played out in ideological symbols instead of medieval romances, and notes that the fantasist, in treating other people as props rather than as minds with wills of their own, is often surprisingly powerful.

The historical example that stays with you is the Italian conquest of Ethiopia. There was no economic or strategic gain to be had, and the diplomatic costs were severe. Ethiopia was a prop in the fantasy pageant of the new Italian Empire. The fantasy demanded that Italy conquer something, and the conquest was an end in itself rather than a means to one. Around it Harris gathers its siblings: the Jacobin dream of reviving the Roman Republic, Mussolini's Roman Empire, the antebellum South's sense of itself as the bearer of true civilisation, and, drawn from the French thinker Georges Sorel, the key insight Mussolini grasped. The Sorelian myth had to be presented as theater, with the audience's willing suspension of disbelief doing the work of persuasion.

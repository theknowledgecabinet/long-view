bigger_picture: 'Lee Harris spends his books naming the myths that organise our lives: the ideologies, the chosen narratives, the stories that make certain things unthinkable and certain acts inevitable. Popper, writing in the middle of a world war, identifies the same mechanism from the methodological side. For Harris the danger is that ideology turns people into characters in a fantasy. For Popper the danger is that historicism turns history into a prophecy. Both are myths, both mobilise, and both treat the present as a means to an imagined end. What each adds to the other is the answer. Harris helps us see the psychology and the culture; Popper helps us see the institutions. Between the two, we can tell where a myth is operating and how a free society might hold it at bay, by keeping the future open and by letting every argument be checked, criticised, and corrected before it is made law.'
bigger_heading: The bigger picture
other_side: 'It is worth knowing who Popper''s first enemy is, because he places him in Athens rather than in a modern party. He reads Plato as the founder of the great historicist project. Confronted with the disorder of change, Plato''s answer was to arrest it: to build a fixed state, run by philosopher kings, where the individual exists to serve the whole. Popper is generous where he can be. He praises Plato''s analysis of social change even as he rejects his remedy, and he calls the generation of Athens before the Peloponnesian War the ''Great Generation'', the moment critical discussion was invented and the open society first became possible. Plato and Popper, in other words, faced the same tremor in the ground: the fear that change leads to decay. Plato wanted to stop it. Popper wanted to build institutions that could keep going through it, one correction at a time. Read together, the two make the stakes clear.'
question: If the future really is not written by any law or destiny, what does that ask of the people and the problems in front of you this week? Which small thing, in your own corner, would you be willing to improve, without waiting for a grander plan?

Today's reading is Karl Popper's defence of the open society, and it is a natural counterpoint to Lee Harris, because the two are working on the same ground: what happens when a people organise their lives around a story they believe is already written. Popper gives that story a name and a method. He calls it historicism, 'the doctrine that history is controlled by specific historical or evolutionary laws whose discovery would enable us to prophesy the destiny of man.' The historicist believes the future has already been written, that if we can only read the laws of history we will know where everything is going. And that, Popper argues, is where the most dangerous ideologies come from: they promise a destination and then spend the present in the service of arriving there.

He traces the idea back to its oldest roots. In the first volume he argues that historicism grew 'out of the tribal form of social life', beginning with the doctrine of a chosen people whose destiny was fixed by God, and he finds its two great modern versions in racialism on the right and Marxism on the left. Both, he says, use the same method: an interpretation of history that claims to have found its law, and therefore to have a right to direct the present. That is a remarkable move, and worth sitting with. The promise of a guaranteed future is what makes these ideas so seductive, because it relieves us of a heavier burden: the open, unwritten, for all we know, possibility of what we might do next.

His answer is the one that gives the book its title. Popper calls it the open society: a society organised so that the protection of the citizens' freedom is the state's first duty, and where problems are fixed one by one rather than swept away in a single revolutionary act. He names this piecemeal social engineering, the practice of 'searching for, and fighting against, the greatest and most urgent evils of society, rather than searching for, and fighting for, its greatest ultimate good.' The quietly radical point is that the future is genuinely open. There is no law telling us what must happen. And if that is true, the whole weight of responsibility falls back on us, on the institutions we build and the arguments we are willing to test in public.

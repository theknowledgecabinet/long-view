bigger_picture: The essay is a thread running through a great deal of Western thought. Harris borrows the Stoic image of concentric circles of loyalty from Nussbaum's essay, identifies Rawls's "veil of ignorance" as the real foundation of her position, and takes his closing insight on the Abbé de St. Pierre from Rousseau, who wrote of a man who "labored for imaginary beings, while believing that he was working for his contemporaries". His warning is one worth carrying into the present. The moment a universal vision is instantiated in a specific policy or curriculum, it loses its claim to represent what is universally desired and becomes just another agenda. Any ideal, however cosmopolitan, can become a particularism when the time comes to defend it with other people's children.
bigger_heading: The bigger picture
other_side: 'Nussbaum herself offers the gentler image of the concentric circles, the self, then family, then the local community, then countrymen, with humanity as a whole outside all of them. Her aim is not to sever local loves but to train the student to "recognize humanity wherever she encounters it", undeterred by anything strange. Harris''s complement is the reminder that mutual recognition is not a mechanism for resolving conflict, and that loyalty must eventually settle somewhere. What the two share is a shared refusal of narrowness: each wants education to enlarge the reader''s circle of concern. What differs is whether the widest circle is a place you live, or a standard by which all smaller circles are judged.'
question: Think of the communities you belong to, from family to country to the wider world. Which of them made you most of who you are, and what would you want to pass on to the next generation?

Harris takes on one of the most debated questions in education: should we teach our children to be patriotic, or to be citizens of the world before all else? His answer is not a flat refusal. It is a careful excavation of what the cosmopolitan ideal actually is, and where it can quietly lead. The strongest form of the case, he grants, is that nationality is an accident, while being human is not. We may accidentally be Swiss or Indian, but we are essentially and necessarily human beings, and that is the one group no one can challenge as arbitrary.

He then traces the word to its origin with a lovely twist. When the Greek Diogenes was asked which city he had been brought up in, he answered that he was a "citizen of the world". Harris shows that this was not a tender universalism but the flip side of cynicism. The cynics modelled their lives on dogs, needing little and owing nothing to any city. Diogenes, Harris writes, consents to be a citizen only of a community that never exists, "simply because there is never any danger that such an abstract entity will make a concrete demand on him. The world asks nothing from him; he owes it nothing in return." A charming phrase, he suggests, with a chilling skeleton underneath.

His most original move is what he calls structural antagonism. Imagine two people with exactly the same aims and values, each fully aware that the other shares them. Could they not be at each other's throats? Obviously they could: two people who want the same woman, or the same piece of land. Conflict can arise precisely because the parties recognise one another, not because they fail to. Harris's closing turn is generous rather than gloomy: it may be that patriotism is the indispensable prerequisite of building a higher and more universal perspective, and the real question is not patriotism or cosmopolitanism, but what kind of each. And it is worth ending where he does. The world is a vastly better place, he writes, because it contains people "whose only fault is the desire to make all people as good and reasonable as they themselves are".

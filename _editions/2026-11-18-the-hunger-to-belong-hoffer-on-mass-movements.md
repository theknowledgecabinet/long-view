bigger_picture: Hoffer sits squarely in the orbit Lee Harris has been helping us read. Harris's central worry in Civilisation and Its Enemies is that modern society is being undermined by movements built on myth and resentment rather than reason, and Hoffer is one of the best thinkers for showing how that happens. The idea is worth carrying because it answers a smug assumption we often make, that fervour is always about ideas. It teaches us that much of what moves people is the hunger to belong, which is why a movement is understood through its feeling, not just its slogans.
bigger_heading: The bigger picture
other_side: Alexis de Tocqueville saw the same human hunger from a different end, in the 1830s. Travelling through the young United States, he argued that modern democracy, with its equal and restless crowds, makes people feel small and gives them a longing for a strong, simple cause or leader. His worry was that this restlessness could lead people to surrender freedom willingly, because independence is tiring and asks for effort. Read together, he and Hoffer point at the same need, the desire to belong to something bigger, which civilisation handles better the more it learns to give that need good, gentle channels.

Eric Hoffer's big, humane claim is that mass movements are far more alike than they look. Communism and fascism were bitter enemies, yet he argues they are "interchangeable" in a very telling sense: they draw on the same deep human feeling, and they recruit the same kind of people. It is not really the ideas that move people, Hoffer says, but the feeling of belonging and purpose a movement offers, the chance to fold your separate self into something bigger and feel worthwhile at last.

He is gentle and specific about who is drawn in. The most likely recruits, he says, are the "New Poor", people who have fallen from comfort and now blame others for their fortunes, and the "misfits", those who feel their private life is meaningless and worthless. In every case the underlying experience is the same: a life that feels "irredeemably spoiled". A movement's chief work, he observes, is to "foster, perfect and perpetuate a facility for united action and self-sacrifice".

His most memorable example is Saul, the fierce persecutor of the early Christians, who became Paul, their fiercest preacher. The man hardly changed; the hunger did. And the most unsettling part of the picture is the need for an enemy. Without a "devil" to hate, a movement often wilts, because it is hatred that actually welds the believers together.

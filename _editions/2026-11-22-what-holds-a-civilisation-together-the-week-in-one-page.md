A week in five editions, and they turn out to circle the same question: what holds a civilisation together? Monday opened with Lee Harris taking on the citizen of the world, the cosmopolitan. He traced the idea back to the Greek Diogenes, who called himself a citizen of the world simply because there is never any danger that such an abstract entity will make a concrete demand on him. A charming phrase, Harris shows, with a chilling skeleton underneath: the world asks nothing from you, and you owe it nothing in return. His closing turn was the gentlest line of the week. He suggests that patriotism is the indispensable prerequisite of a higher and more universal view, and he writes that the world is a vastly better place because it contains people whose only fault is the desire to make all people as good and reasonable as they themselves are.

Tuesday gave us Thucydides and the Melian Dialogue, the summer of 416 BC, when the great Athenian empire sent at least 3,400 men and 38 ships to the tiny island of Melos. The small city had stayed neutral in the long war, and Athens gave it the choice of tribute or destruction, with no moral argument at all. The strongest do what they can, the weak suffer what they must. What made the scene unforgettable was not just the lesson, but the choice of the man who wrote it: a historian of Athens let his own city be heard at its most brutal, without a single softening word.

Wednesday was Eric Hoffer, who argued that mass movements are far more alike than they look, even enemies. It is not the ideas that move people so much as the feeling of belonging and purpose a movement offers, the chance to fold your separate self into something bigger. His most memorable example was Saul, the fierce persecutor of the early Christians, who became Paul, their fiercest preacher. The man hardly changed, Hoffer says, the hunger did.

Thursday looked under the hood at how we judge: moral foundations theory, the idea that morality rests on a small set of instincts, care and fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and liberty. The famous finding is that the political camps feel so far apart because members of one are, to a degree, blind to one or more of the moral foundations of the others. The proposed remedy was charmingly concrete: walk around with all the foundations switched on, and the people on the far side of the wall stop looking like villains and start looking like neighbours with different priorities.

Friday closed the week with Harris on common sense. A team of psychologists at Cornell worried that most voters cannot tell a brilliant idea from a bad one. Harris had two answers. Extraordinarily clever people have held extraordinarily dumb ideas, so intelligence is no protection. And the best hope for democracy still lies in the unregulated marketplace of ideas, with the old caution: let the buyer beware.

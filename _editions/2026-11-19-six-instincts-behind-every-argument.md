bigger_picture: 'This idea sits in the conversation Lee Harris kept for much of his career about what groups do to the minds of their members: why loyalty to one''s own tribe feels less like a preference than a moral duty. Moral foundations names that loyalty and shows how it binds, with the binding foundations of loyalty, authority and sanctity uniting a group against the wider world, while the individualizing foundations of care and fairness check the impulse from inside. It also deepens the elephant and rider picture from The Righteous Mind, where the elephant is the quick moral intuition and the rider the explanation we offer afterwards. It is worth carrying around today because it suggests that a great deal of what looks like stubbornness is two people caring about different, genuine moral things.'
bigger_heading: The bigger picture
other_side: 'The anthropologist Richard Shweder, whose work inspired Haidt, approached the same question from the other end. Rather than asking which moral feelings are innate, he asked what different cultures build with them, and found that societies organise their moral life around ''three distinct but coherent clusters of moral concerns'': the ethics of autonomy, community and divinity. Shweder supplied the cultural map and Haidt the psychology, and the two agree on the most useful point of all: morality is plural and deeply felt, and understanding a community often starts with noticing which foundations it takes most seriously.'
question: 'The next time a disagreement feels stuck, try a small experiment: before answering, name the moral foundation the other person is speaking from. Is the argument really about the same thing, or are two different foundations talking past each other?'

Moral foundations theory, proposed in 2004 by the psychologists Jonathan Haidt and Craig Joseph and later expanded with colleagues, suggests that morality is not one thing, but rests on a small set of innate, modular foundations. What began as five grew to six: care and harm, fairness and cheating, loyalty and betrayal, authority and subversion, sanctity and degradation, and liberty and oppression. Haidt and his colleagues describe care and fairness as an 'individualizing cluster', and loyalty, authority and sanctity as a 'group-focused binding cluster'.

The theory grew out of what Haidt called moral dumbfounding: the experience of feeling that an act is wrong while being unable to say why. For Haidt, these moments support the idea that emotion drives moral judgement. In his formulation, moral judgement is 'caused by quick moral intuitions', and the reasoning we offer afterwards is largely a rationalization of a judgement that was already made.

The application that made the theory famous concerns politics. Measured with the Moral Foundations Questionnaire, progressives are most sensitive to care and fairness, conservatives to all the foundations, and libertarians to liberty, and Haidt and his colleagues suggest this is why the camps feel so far apart: members of one camp are 'to a degree, blind to one or more of the moral foundations of the others'. The proposed remedy is charmingly concrete. Picture politics as a wall, with the foundations as doorways, and spend some time walking around with all five switched on, and the people on the far side of the wall stop looking like villains and start looking like neighbours with different priorities.

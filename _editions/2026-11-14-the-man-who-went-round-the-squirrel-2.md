bigger_picture: 'Lee Harris spent his career applying this same method to politics. When he defined civilisation in Civilization and Its Enemies, he refused to make it an abstraction and instead described what a civilised society does in concrete life: it tolerates, it cooperates, it lessens its reliance on violence, and it keeps its gains across generations. That is James''s pragmatic method carried into the life of peoples: we judge a way of living by its fruits, not by its principles on paper. It is still a useful habit today, because so many public arguments, and many private ones, are fought over words no one can actually point to. If you cannot say what would be different, you may be arguing about the label rather than about the world.'
bigger_heading: The bigger picture
other_side: 'James sketches the idealist''s temper in this very lecture, and it deserves a moment. The rationalist, he writes, is ''comfortable only in the presence of abstractions'', and for the rationalist truth is ''an absolute correspondence of our thoughts with an equally absolute reality'', what we ought to think unconditionally. That demand is a real protection against drift, because if anything that works is true, so is any convenient fable. The two temperaments form a quiet pair: the pragmatist asks what an idea does in the world, and the idealist asks what it must be to deserve our belief, and each catches the other whenever it strays.'

It begins with a scene. Some years earlier, James writes, he was with a camping party in the mountains and returned from a solitary ramble to find everyone 'engaged in a ferocious metaphysical dispute'. A man is pacing round a tree, and a squirrel on the tree keeps turning, so the trunk always stays between them. Does the man go round the squirrel? Everyone has taken sides, the numbers are even, and both sides appeal to James to make a majority. His answer is not a verdict. It is a distinction. The answer, he says, 'depends on what you practically mean' by the words: if you mean passing from the north of the squirrel to the east, then the south, then the west, the man obviously goes round; but if you mean being first in front of him, then on his right, then behind him, then on his left, the man fails to go round, because the squirrel keeps its belly turned towards him all the while. 'You are both right and both wrong', he concludes, according as you conceive the verb in one practical fashion or the other.

That little anecdote is the whole method, and it is a charming one. James calls it the pragmatic method, 'primarily a method of settling metaphysical disputes that otherwise might be interminable'. Take any idea and ask what difference would it practically make to anyone if this notion rather than that notion were true. If no practical difference whatever can be traced, 'then the alternatives mean practically the same thing, and all dispute is idle'. He says the term is derived from the Greek word pragma, meaning action, from which 'practice' and 'practical' come, and traces the principle to the American logician Charles Peirce, who in 1878 pointed out that our beliefs are really rules for action, and that to develop a thought's meaning we need only determine 'what conduct it is fitted to produce'.

Here James moves on to the theory of truth, 'the very central point' of the doctrine. Truth, he says, is 'one species of good', and 'the true is the name of whatever proves itself to be good in the way of belief, and good, too, for definite, assignable reasons'. Yet he is generous even to grand systems. Believers in the Absolute of idealism, he allows, have 'a right ever and anon to take a moral holiday, to let the world wag in its own way'. And he knows that beliefs guard one another: 'the greatest enemy of any one of our truths may be the rest of our truths'. A new truth, he says, grows much as a tree grows, grafting itself on to the ancient body of what we already believe. Pragmatism, in his words, is a 'mediator and reconciler' that 'unstiffens our theories'.
